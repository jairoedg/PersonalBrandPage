**Company:** Comverse (later Mavenir)  
**Client:** Entel Peru  
**Period:** January 2014 – December 2014  
**Role:** Senior Project Lead — Presales Support / On-site Implementation Lead  
**Industry:** Telecommunications  
**Country:** Peru  
**Delivery Model:** Waterfall  
**Status:** Production Go-Live Completed

---

# Executive Summary

Entel entered the Peruvian telecommunications market in 2014 as part of a major strategic expansion from Chile into Peru.

The company acquired an existing local operation and launched a broad technology transformation to modernize its platforms and support a new 4G mobile network.

Comverse participated as the Value Added Services provider, delivering services including VMS, USSD, and SMS.

My role began before implementation, supporting scope definition, delivery planning, and timeline discussions during the presales stage.

After contract approval, I continued as the on-site implementation lead, coordinating customer stakeholders, Comverse teams, architects, offshore development, and external providers.

This project represented an important step in my evolution from implementation leadership toward broader delivery leadership because I became involved not only in execution, but also in scope definition, planning, negotiation, multi-vendor coordination, and delivery recovery.

# Delivery Challenges

The project experienced several important delivery challenges.

## Software Delivery Disruption

A strike in Israel delayed delivery of software required to begin and continue testing activities.

This created direct schedule risk.

Rather than waiting for the complete software package, the team needed to identify ways to continue progressing while maintaining transparency with the customer.

---

## Testing Pressure

Delayed software releases reduced the available testing window.

Once software became available, the project needed to accelerate:

- Functional testing.
- Defect identification.
- Defect resolution.
- Retesting.
- Release validation.

Customer participation became critical to recovering schedule.

---

## Multi-Team Coordination

The implementation involved:

- Customer teams.
- Comverse implementation resources.
- Architects.
- Offshore development.
- Testers.
- Third-party technology providers.

Maintaining alignment across these groups was essential.

---

# Leadership Decisions

## 1. Maintain Transparent Communication

The customer was informed clearly about the impact of the software delays and the resulting schedule risks.

The objective was to avoid surprises and preserve confidence despite circumstances outside the immediate implementation team's control.

---

## 2. Advance Non-Critical-Path Work

While waiting for delayed software, the team advanced activities that were not on the immediate critical path but were still required before go-live.

This reduced idle time and protected future milestones.

---

## 3. Negotiate Incremental Software Releases

Rather than waiting for the entire software solution, I worked with the development team to organize partial releases by feature.

This allowed testing to begin incrementally.

The approach created shorter feedback cycles and allowed the team to:

- Test available functionality earlier.
- Identify defects sooner.
- Return issues to development.
- Receive updated releases.
- Repeat the cycle until the complete solution was ready.

---

## 4. Increase Testing Capacity

I coordinated with the customer to increase testing resources once software became available.

The goal was to use the available time more effectively and recover part of the schedule lost during the strike.

---

## 5. Keep the Customer Working Toward the Same Goal

A key objective was ensuring that the customer remained actively engaged in solving the problem rather than becoming focused only on the delay.

The delivery approach depended on close collaboration between customer, implementation, and development teams.

# Leadership Reflection

This project reinforced that project success is not determined by the absence of problems. Recovery plans are often more valuable than perfect plans.

The project experienced a major disruption outside the implementation team's direct control, but the delivery still needed to move forward.

The most important lesson was that a delivery leader should keep looking for alternatives rather than waiting for ideal conditions.

Incremental software releases, increased testing capacity, and transparent customer communication helped reduce the impact of the delay and maintain momentum.

Looking back today, I recognize that flexibility in delivery planning can be one of the strongest tools available to a delivery leader. This project also reinforced the value of working with the customer as one delivery team rather than treating problems as supplier-versus-client issues.

---

# What I Would Do Differently Today

Today I would formalize contingency planning earlier.

For a project with significant offshore development and external dependencies, I would define alternative delivery scenarios before execution.

These could include:

- Partial software releases.
- Prioritized feature delivery.
- Alternative test sequencing.
- Increased testing capacity.
- Predefined escalation paths.
- Contingency plans for supplier or labor disruptions.

This would make it easier to switch delivery strategy quickly when external events affect the project.

---

# How This Project Changed the Way I Lead

- When delivery conditions change, I look for alternatives rather than waiting for the original plan to recover.
- Transparent communication protects customer confidence during uncertainty.
- Incremental delivery can reduce risk and create earlier feedback.
- Customer participation is essential when schedule recovery requires rapid testing and decision-making.
- Multi-vendor projects require active coordination rather than passive dependency tracking.
- Delivery recovery works best when customer and supplier teams remain aligned around the same objective.
