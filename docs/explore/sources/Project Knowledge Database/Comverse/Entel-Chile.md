# Entel Chile – Project Recovery and Customer Confidence Restoration

**Company:** Comverse (later Mavenir)  
**Client:** Entel Chile  
**Period:** Jan 2015 - June 2015
**Role:** Senior Project Lead — Project Recovery / On-site Delivery Lead  
**Industry:** Telecommunications  
**Country:** Chile  
**Delivery Model:** Waterfall  
**Status:** Production Go-Live Completed

---

# Executive Summary

Entel launched major telecommunications transformation initiatives in Chile and Peru at approximately the same time.

While the Peru engagement supported Entel's market expansion into a new country, the Chile program involved an established telecommunications operator with a significant customer base and existing market position.

After completing my work in Peru, I was relocated to Chile because the Chilean project had accumulated significant delays and the customer had lost confidence in the engagement.

My mission was to understand the actual state of the project, rebuild a credible delivery plan, restore customer engagement, secure stronger internal technical support, and move the solution into production.

This became one of the most important project-recovery experiences of my Comverse career.

---

# Business Context

Entel was executing strategic technology-transformation programs in both Chile and Peru.

The two initiatives shared similar strategic objectives and technology foundations, but the business context was different.

In Peru, Entel was entering a new market.

In Chile, Entel was already an established telecommunications provider with a significant subscriber base and a strong market position.

The Chilean project therefore affected an existing and mature operating environment.

---

# Business Problem

By the time I joined the project, the central problem was no longer simply technical delivery.

The project had experienced significant delays.

Customer confidence had deteriorated.

The customer had become pessimistic about the likelihood of successful completion and was increasingly reluctant to assign its own resources to support testing and delivery activities.

This created a difficult cycle:

- Delays reduced customer confidence.
- Reduced confidence lowered customer participation.
- Lower participation made recovery more difficult.
- Continued lack of progress further damaged confidence.

The project needed both delivery recovery and relationship recovery.

---

# Why I Was Assigned

After completing the Entel Peru implementation, Comverse decided to use my experience with the same customer organization and similar technology environment to support recovery of the Chile engagement.

Although the stakeholders were different, the Peru project gave me relevant context about:

- Entel's organization.
- The technology solution.
- Delivery dependencies.
- Customer expectations.
- Internal Comverse teams.

My mission was to move the project back toward a realistic path to production.

# Delivery Challenges

## Loss of Customer Confidence

The biggest challenge was not a single technical problem.

It was the customer's declining confidence in the project.

The customer was increasingly skeptical that the project could be completed successfully.

This affected its willingness to allocate resources and actively support delivery.

---

## Existing Delays

The project already had a history of missed expectations.

Any new recovery plan therefore needed to be credible.

Simply presenting another optimistic schedule would have been unlikely to regain stakeholder confidence.

---

## Technical Issue Resolution

The project still had unresolved technical issues that required access to higher-level engineering and support teams.

Quick technical progress was necessary to demonstrate that the recovery effort was different from previous attempts.

---

# Leadership Decisions

## 1. Understand the Real Situation Before Replanning

My first action was to assess the project as it actually existed.

I reviewed:

- Progress completed.
- Remaining scope.
- Open technical issues.
- Delivery risks.
- Customer concerns.
- Resource availability.
- Dependencies.

The priority was to establish an objective baseline before proposing a new plan.

---

## 2. Accept the Existing Failure Rather Than Defend the Old Plan

I did not try to preserve the original delivery dates simply because they had already been committed.

The project needed a new plan based on the actual situation.

The recovery approach therefore accepted the existing delays and established a more realistic delivery path.

---

## 3. Rebuild the Delivery Plan

The original expectation of completing the project in December 2014 was reassessed.

A revised plan targeted completion around May 2015.

The goal was to create a schedule that the team could actually execute and the customer could believe.

---

## 4. Secure Stronger Internal Support

I raised the situation internally within Comverse and requested immediate support from the teams able to resolve the project's most difficult technical issues.

This included:

- Software Development.
- Architecture.
- Tier 4 Support.
- Senior internal leadership.

The objective was to reduce issue-resolution time and demonstrate visible progress.

---

## 5. Create Quick Wins

The recovery strategy focused on progressing achievable milestones and resolving issues that could provide visible evidence that the project was moving again.

These quick wins were important not only for the schedule, but also for rebuilding customer confidence.

---

## 6. Restore Customer Participation

The project could not recover without active customer involvement.

A major part of the work therefore involved re-engaging customer stakeholders and demonstrating through delivery progress that continued participation was worthwhile.

# Leadership Reflection

This project fundamentally changed my understanding of recovery leadership.

When a project has been delayed repeatedly, the main issue may no longer be the plan itself.

The relationship around the plan may also be damaged.

In this situation, the customer had lost confidence and reduced its own participation.

That meant the recovery strategy needed to address both execution and trust.

I learned that rebuilding confidence requires more than communication. It requires visible evidence that the delivery organization is taking corrective action.

The revised plan needed to be credible. Internal technical support needed to improve. Issues needed to be resolved faster. Milestones needed to be achieved consistently.

Only then could the customer reasonably believe that the project was moving toward success again.

Leadership means accepting reality before defining the next plan.

---

# What I Would Do Differently Today

Today I would establish earlier indicators that customer confidence was deteriorating.

I would track signals such as:

- Reduced customer participation.
- Repeated missed commitments.
- Slower customer decision-making.
- Increased skepticism in status meetings.
- Delayed testing support.
- Escalation frequency.
- Lack of confidence in revised dates.

Those indicators would provide earlier visibility that recovery actions were required before the relationship became critical.

I would also establish a formal recovery governance model earlier, with:

- A clearly agreed baseline.
- Explicit issue ownership.
- Strong technical escalation paths.
- Measurable recovery milestones.
- Customer participation commitments.
- Regular review of confidence and delivery progress.

---

# How This Project Changed the Way I Lead

- When the client relationship has deteriorated, restoring confidence in the project becomes a primary delivery objective.
- Confidence is rebuilt through visible corrective action, not promises alone.
- When reality changes, I reassess the plan rather than defend an obsolete baseline.
- Recovery plans should be based on the actual project situation and agreed with stakeholders.
- Strong escalation is necessary when the teams capable of resolving critical issues are outside the immediate project structure.
- Customer participation needs to be actively recovered when confidence has declined.
- Consistently meeting revised commitments is one of the strongest ways to rebuild credibility.
