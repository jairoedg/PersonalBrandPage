Leadership

Leadership philosophy

Projects succeed because people succeed.

Technology alone does not solve business problems.

People do.

My responsibility is to create an environment where engineers can focus on building quality solutions, stakeholders understand what is happening, risks are managed proactively and communication becomes a competitive advantage rather than a source of uncertainty.

Success is measured not only by delivering on time, but by delivering solutions organizations trust, adopt and value.

Leadership principles

  1. 01

    Projects succeed because people succeed.

    At Avianca I created the conditions for the right people to own the technical decisions. At DCTX I treated team fatigue as a risk to manage when overtime was agreed, not something to absorb. At DFA, when the team struggled with the gRPC architecture, I brought in an experienced developer who could turn the architectural intent into practical guidance, which helped the team stabilize its delivery.

    Stories: Avianca, DCTX, DFA

  2. 02

    Communication uncertainty is often a greater delivery risk than technical uncertainty.

    At DCTX, when AI-generated user stories didn't match the technical context, a new BA was brought in to bridge the gap between Dev and Product teams. At GSK I set up a clear communication path, so requirements, issues, dependencies, and changes were understood across SAP, business, integration, and provider teams. At Entel Chile I re-established customer communication alongside a credible revised plan, and the customer's participation improved as we met the revised milestones.

    Stories: DCTX, GSK, Entel Chile

  3. 03

    Removing unnecessary friction allows technical teams to perform at their highest level.

    At Entel Peru, the strike in Israel held up the software my team needed, so I agreed partial releases with the developers. That kept testing moving and let us advance work that wasn't on the critical path. At DCTX I adapted our delivery workflow to the product team's parallel feature model. At Entel Chile, the customer had stopped assigning its own people to testing. Once we rebuilt the plan and showed visible progress, its participation came back and the work could move again.

    Stories: Entel Peru, DCTX, Entel Chile

  4. 04

    Technology creates value only when it solves real business problems.

    At Avianca I helped build the business case and Total Cost of Ownership model before any build decision, so the migration was justified by a business need before funding. At Entel Peru, during presales, I took part in scope and technical and commercial discussions with the customer.

    Stories: Avianca, Entel Peru

  5. 05

    Predictability builds trust.

    At Claro Peru I owned the schedule and kept delay discussions to facts. At Entel Chile I rebuilt the plan around milestones the team could meet, and the customer's participation came back as we delivered them. At GT&T, weekly status reports kept progress and risks visible, because predictable delivery comes from disciplined communication.

    Stories: Claro Peru, Entel Chile, GT&T

  6. 06

    Every difficult project leaves a lesson worth documenting.

    At Tigo, the hardest lesson was that a large program needs program-level ownership and early escalation. Today I would pilot one process before scaling, prove the architecture, and validate the data first. At DCTX I learned that I should have challenged the delivery model itself, pushing management and product leadership to set milestones in the product roadmap, each tied to features that could be signed off. Instead of one big release at the end, the work should have been split into deliverables along that roadmap.

    Stories: Tigo, DCTX

  7. 07

    Supporting people also means making timely decisions when additional support is no longer helping the individual, the team or the project.

    At Tigo I kept trying to make a large team work before the product, the infrastructure, and the architecture were clear, and I realized too late that some teams lacked the capability the work required. Today I would change the structure before scaling, as soon as capability is in doubt. At DFA I delayed a rotation decision for a team member who was not performing as expected, which affected delivery. I learned that some decisions should be taken immediately.

    Stories: Tigo, DFA
