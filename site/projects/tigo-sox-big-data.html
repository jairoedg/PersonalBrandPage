Tigo: SOX Big Data Automation Program

TCS · Telecommunications · December 2018 – October 2019

Tigo initiated a strategic SOX compliance program following Millicom's listing on Nasdaq.

The objective was to strengthen internal controls and reduce financial risk by automating approximately 25 important SOX control processes using a centralized Big Data platform.

I was responsible for coordinating the construction of the Big Data infrastructure jointly with Tigo and for leading delivery across the individual SOX process solutions.

The engagement included a product team in Colombia, architecture support, and several development teams in India.

This became one of the most difficult projects of my career.

The original delivery model did not produce the expected results and was stopped after approximately six months.

Looking back, I can identify significant mistakes in how I led the engagement, particularly around architecture governance, delivery strategy, delegation, escalation, Agile adoption, and recognizing that the initiative required program-level rather than project-level management.

This experience became an important turning point in how I approached complex delivery afterward.

Outcome: Not achieved: the planned SOX control monitoring through Tableau dashboards was not delivered, and the original approach was stopped after approximately six months and restructured.

Business Context

Tigo initiated a strategic program to strengthen SOX compliance controls.

The initiative was aligned with Millicom's increased regulatory obligations following its Nasdaq listing.

TCS was contracted to build a Big Data solution intended to automate important SOX control monitoring through Tableau dashboard fed by data sourced in a Data Warehouse plus external non-SQL files, such as, logs, networking measurements, scripts and excel spreadsheets files with business data.

The target concept was to:

  1. Collect data from multiple operational systems.
  2. Centralize relevant information in a Data Warehouse and Big Data environment.
  3. Ingest and transform the data.
  4. Apply the rules required by each SOX process.
  5. Produce outputs and reports supporting compliance controls.

Approximately 25 SOX processes were included in the initial scope.

Scope of Responsibility

My role combined elements of project and program delivery management.

My responsibilities included:

  • Coordinating construction of the Big Data platform with Tigo.
  • Coordinating product definition for approximately 25 SOX processes.
  • Coordinating architecture discussions.
  • Leading a product team in Colombia.
  • Coordinating multiple development teams located in India.
  • Managing risks, issues, dependencies, and delivery progress.
  • Coordinating with Tigo project and analytics leadership.
  • Understanding of the SOX process solutions.

At the time, I treated the initiative largely as a project.

In retrospect, the size, number of teams, technical dependencies, and number of products being developed meant that it required a stronger program-management approach.

Delivery Organization

The delivery structure included approximately:

  • 1 product team in Colombia.
  • 4 Business Analysts.
  • 2 Architects.
  • Approximately 25 Big Data developers.
  • 2 Scrum Masters.
  • Multiple distributed development teams in India.

Key stakeholders included:

  • Tigo Project Manager.
  • Tigo Head of Analytics.
  • Tigo business-process stakeholders.
  • TCS Delivery leadership.
  • TCS development leadership in India.

Technology Landscape

The solution involved:

  • Big Data technologies.
  • Spark.
  • Hive.
  • MySQL.
  • Data Warehouse technologies.
  • Data ingestion pipelines.
  • Data transformation and reporting (Tableau).

Major Delivery Problems

Several structural problems emerged during the engagement.

1. Insufficient Big Data Expertise

Although TCS was positioned as the technology provider, the delivery organization did not consistently have the level of Big Data experience required for the program.

This affected architecture decisions, development quality, and technical execution.

I recognized that the team did not have sufficient knowledge, but I did not escalate the issue firmly enough or early enough.

2. Incomplete Understanding of Source Data

Some SOX processes depended on information whose source, quality, ownership, or collection method was not sufficiently understood.

Some required data was also dependent on manual human activities.

Although several data sources were successfully ingested into the platform, the solution did not consistently contain the complete and reliable information required to execute the targeted SOX controls.

3. Architecture Was Not Standardized

A common Big Data architecture was never established strongly enough across the program.

Different development teams implemented different technical approaches, programming languages, and solution patterns for individual SOX processes.

This created inconsistency and made the overall solution harder to govern and scale.

I understood that stronger architectural direction was necessary but did not force the issue or escalate it effectively.

4. Weak Product Definition

The team had limited business understanding of the SOX processes.

Requirements and user stories were therefore not consistently ready for development.

The lack of strong product-management practices affected:

  • Requirements elicitation.
  • Story quality.
  • Definition of deliverables.
  • Development-team readiness.

5. Limited Agile Experience

This was one of my first substantial Agile delivery experiences.

My limited Scrum and Agile experience affected how I structured and managed the work.

An Agile specialist was eventually requested, but the engagement had already accumulated significant delivery problems.

I later recognized that simply introducing Agile practices could not compensate for unresolved problems in architecture, product definition, skills, and delivery structure.

6. Development Capability Gaps

Some development teams in India had limited experience with the type of Big Data engineering required.

This became particularly visible when low-performing data pipelines began to be delivered.

I recognized this problem too late and did not escalate strongly enough to replace, restructure, or reinforce those teams.

7. Distributed-Team Communication

The Colombia and India teams operated with different contexts and communication patterns.

Language and Time zones coordination difficulties further affected delivery progress.

The distributed operating model required much stronger alignment than I established at the time.

Where My Leadership Fell Short

This is one of the few engagements in my career where I do not feel that I responded effectively enough to the problems I could see.

I identified many of the risks and reported them, but reporting a risk is not the same as taking the actions required to resolve it.

Several areas stand out.

Architecture

I knew the project lacked sufficient architectural consistency.

I did not escalate strongly enough or insist that a common architecture be established before allowing multiple teams to continue independently.

Skills

I realized too late that some development teams did not have the capabilities required for the work.

I continued trying to make the existing structure work rather than forcing a decision about team capability.

Program Structure

I tried to manage too much of the initiative directly.

I did not break the work down into sufficiently independent areas with clear ownership and accountability.

Delegation

I accepted responsibility for decisions and activities that belonged to other stakeholders, including some client responsibilities and decisions made by senior management or commercial teams.

This increased the amount of operational pressure I was personally carrying while reducing clarity around accountability.

Escalation

Although I reported risks and issues, I did not take a strong enough position when I believed the delivery model itself was not viable.

I needed to be clearer about what could and could not reasonably be delivered under the existing conditions.

Outcome

After approximately six months of poor delivery performance, the original project approach was stopped.

The expected SOX control results had not been delivered under the initial model.

The delivery approach and scope were subsequently changed.

A new team was established directly in Colombia, and the focus shifted toward delivering the most important SOX processes before Tigo's required deadline.

Some priority processes were eventually released under this revised approach.

The original delivery model, however, did not achieve its intended objectives.

What I Learned

This project produced some of the most important lessons of my career.

Delegate Deliberately

Large initiatives cannot be managed by concentrating responsibility in one person.

Clear ownership is required across product, architecture, development, customer responsibilities, and delivery management.

Separate Accountability

Client responsibilities, supplier responsibilities, management decisions, and delivery-team responsibilities must remain explicit.

Supporting the wider program does not mean accepting accountability for every decision.

Escalation Requires a Position

Reporting that a team lacks the necessary skills is not enough.

When delivery conditions are no longer viable, a delivery leader needs to state clearly:

  • what is wrong,
  • what the impact will be,
  • what decision is required,
  • and what needs to change.

Architecture Must Precede Scale

Multiple teams should not independently build solutions when the common architecture has not been established.

The absence of a standardized architecture created unnecessary technical inconsistency across the program.

Validate Data Before Building Solutions

For a data-driven program, the availability, quality, ownership, and structure of the required data must be understood before committing to large-scale implementation.

Program Structure Matters

Twenty-five related products delivered by several distributed teams required a program-level operating model.

Trying to manage the initiative as one large project increased complexity and reduced clarity.

What I Would Do Differently Today

The biggest change I would make would be to avoid launching approximately 25 SOX processes simultaneously.

I would begin with a pilot.

Step 1 — Select One Representative SOX Process

Choose one process with enough complexity to test:

  • source-data availability,
  • architecture,
  • ingestion,
  • transformation,
  • control logic,
  • reporting,
  • product definition,
  • development capability,
  • and deployment.

Step 2 — Prove the Architecture

Before scaling, establish a common technical architecture and confirm that it works end to end.

Step 3 — Validate Data Readiness

Identify:

  • required sources,
  • ownership,
  • data quality,
  • manual dependencies,
  • missing information,
  • and ingestion feasibility.

Step 4 — Establish the Product Model

Create clear requirements, acceptance criteria, and ownership before development begins.

Step 5 — Validate Team Capability

Confirm that the teams have the skills required to build and operate the solution.

Where capability is insufficient, escalate and change the delivery structure before scaling.

Step 6 — Scale Only After the Pilot Works

Once one process proves the delivery model, replicate the approach across additional SOX processes.

Step 7 — Establish Program Governance

Break the initiative into clearly owned workstreams with explicit responsibilities across:

  • product,
  • architecture,
  • development,
  • data,
  • client activities,
  • and delivery management.

How This Project Changed the Way I Lead

This project changed several aspects of my leadership.

I learned that identifying risks is not enough if I am unwilling to take a clear position when the project needs a structural change.

I learned that I should not accept responsibility simply because an issue is important.

Responsibility must remain with the person or organization capable of making the required decision.

I learned that delegation is not giving work away; it is creating clear accountability.

I learned that technical architecture and data readiness need to be proven before scaling a complex data program.

I learned that a large initiative with multiple products and delivery teams must be structured and governed as a program.

Most importantly, I learned that when I believe the current delivery model cannot succeed, I need to stand my ground, present the evidence, escalate clearly, and request the decision needed to change it.

Evidence

I do not consider this engagement a delivery success.

Its value in my professional history comes primarily from the lessons it created and the changes it produced in how I approached later engagements.

Concrete facts that can still be evidenced include:

  • Leadership of a large distributed delivery organization.
  • Coordination of approximately 25 SOX process solutions.
  • Exposure to Big Data architecture and data-engineering delivery.
  • Coordination between Colombia and India teams.
  • Experience working with SOX-related business processes.
  • Experience identifying limitations in architecture, data readiness, team capability, and delivery structure.
  • Participation in the restructuring of the delivery approach after the original model failed.

This project should therefore be represented as a major learning experience rather than as a successful case study.
