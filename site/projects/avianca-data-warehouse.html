Avianca: Azure Data Warehouse Migration Definition

TCS · Aviation · January 2021 – June 2021

Client
Avianca Holdings
Role
Technical Project Manager / Client Partner
Domain
Data & Analytics / Cloud Transformation
Delivery stage
Inception / Due Diligence / Project Definition
Technology
Microsoft Azure, Oracle Exadata, Databricks, Synapse, Python, Spark, MySQL

Avianca initiated the definition of a cloud transformation initiative to modernize its analytics platform and migrate from an on-premises Oracle Exadata Data Warehouse toward a Microsoft Azure-based data architecture.

The objective was to improve the organization's ability to respond to market and operational information needs while creating a technology foundation capable of supporting new data sources, higher data velocity, and emerging capabilities such as Machine Learning, Big Data, Artificial Intelligence, and IoT.

My participation was limited to the initial project-definition stage.

I worked with Avianca analytics stakeholders, Microsoft consultants, and TCS delivery leadership to develop the business case, Total Cost of Ownership model, strategic roadmap, and initial migration planning required to support project funding and future execution.

I also led small Machine Learning and AI proof-of-concept initiatives using Databricks and Synapse to explore potential improvements to aeronautical processes.

My time on the engagement ended before the full migration strategy, target architecture, final scope, and delivery plan were completed because I moved to Endava.

Outcome: Business case and TCO completed for sponsor approval. See note

Business Context

Avianca Holdings relied on an on-premises Oracle Exadata Data Warehouse to support analytics and business intelligence.

The organization identified the need to modernize this environment to improve its ability to respond to changing market and operational information requirements.

The desired future state included a cloud-based analytics platform capable of supporting:

  • New internal and external data sources.
  • Different types of structured and unstructured data.
  • Higher data velocity.
  • Real-time and near-real-time analytics.
  • Machine Learning.
  • Big Data.
  • Artificial Intelligence.
  • IoT-related use cases.

Relevant data sources included Avianca Holdings systems and external platforms such as:

  • Amadeus.
  • IATA.
  • Social media.
  • Other operational and market-information sources.

Business Problem

The existing Oracle Exadata-based analytics solution constrained Avianca's ability to respond quickly to new information demands.

The current environment also made it more difficult to adopt emerging data capabilities and scale analytics efficiently.

The business risks included:

  • Slower time-to-insight.
  • Reduced ability to support new business use cases.
  • Difficulty scaling concurrent analytics demand.
  • Higher infrastructure costs relative to delivered value.
  • Slower adoption of Machine Learning, Big Data, AI, and IoT capabilities.

The project therefore needed to define a credible migration path from the existing on-premises platform to a modern Azure-based analytics architecture.

Biggest Challenges

1. First Cloud Transformation Experience

This was my first major engagement involving cloud migration and Azure analytics.

I needed to contribute to project definition while simultaneously learning:

  • Cloud migration concepts.
  • Azure analytics capabilities.
  • Data modernization approaches.
  • Cost-modeling requirements.
  • Migration-planning considerations.

2. Limited Airline-Domain Knowledge

I was also new to the airline industry.

Understanding how Avianca used its data, how the existing Data Warehouse supported the business, and how external sources such as Amadeus and IATA contributed to analytics required additional learning.

3. Project Funding Documentation

The project required a business case and Total Cost of Ownership model before funding could be approved.

I had limited previous experience building these artifacts for a cloud-data transformation initiative.

This required close collaboration with Microsoft and Avianca stakeholders.

4. Missing Data Architect

One of the biggest delivery constraints was the lack of a Data Architect responsible for making and validating the technical decisions required for the target solution.

Without that role, I was repeatedly exposed to technical decisions that were outside the level of authority and expertise appropriate for my position.

This became a critical issue because the project could not complete areas such as:

  • Target architecture.
  • Final migration strategy.
  • Scope definition.
  • Cost estimation.
  • Delivery sequencing.

without clear technical ownership.

Leadership Decisions

1. Reuse Lessons From the Previous Big Data Engagement

Although this was my first Azure migration initiative, I used lessons from the previous Big Data program to identify risks earlier.

The Tigo – SOX Big Data Automation Program experience had shown me the consequences of proceeding without sufficient architecture, clear ownership, or validated technical capability.

This made me more willing to raise concerns during the definition stage rather than waiting for them to become delivery problems.

2. Create a Table of Experts

Rather than trying to make the technical decisions myself, I brought together specialists from:

  • Avianca.
  • Microsoft.
  • TCS.
  • Data engineering teams.

The objective was to create a shared decision-making forum for defining the migration strategy and resolving technical questions.

3. Escalate the Need for a Data Architect

I explicitly raised the need for a Data Architect capable of owning the critical technical definitions.

This was an important change from my previous project.

Instead of silently absorbing technical responsibility beyond my role, I identified the missing ownership as a delivery risk.

4. Base Decisions on Available Expertise

Where decisions had to be made, I relied on:

  • Microsoft technical guidance.
  • Avianca's knowledge of the existing analytics environment.
  • My previous Big Data delivery experience.
  • The input of available Data Engineers.

This allowed the project-definition work to continue while making clear that final architecture ownership still required the appropriate specialist role.

Leadership Reflection

This project reinforced the importance of delegating responsibilities to the people with the right expertise and accountability.

Working with Avianca stakeholders, Microsoft specialists, and the data engineering team helped me understand that my role as Technical Project Manager was not to make every technical decision myself, but to create the conditions for the right people to make those decisions at the right time.

I was not the deepest technical expert in the room, and I did not need to be. My responsibility was to connect the stakeholders, clarify ownership, identify missing roles, and keep the project-definition work moving from a strategic delivery perspective.

This was an important evolution from my previous Big Data project. In that engagement, I accepted too much responsibility for decisions that should have belonged to architects, technical specialists, or other stakeholders.

In the Avianca project, I was more deliberate about separating responsibilities and escalating the absence of a Data Architect instead of absorbing that accountability myself.

The experience strengthened one of the principles I now apply consistently: effective leadership is not about owning every decision; it is about making sure every important decision has a clear owner with the right expertise.

What I Would Do Differently Today

Today I would formalize the project-definition stage more strongly before moving toward execution. I would ensure that the following were completed or highly-defined before committing to a Project migration plan:

  • Target architecture.
  • Data-source inventory.
  • Data-quality assessment.
  • Migration patterns.
  • Workload prioritization.
  • Cost model.
  • Security and governance requirements.
  • Operating model.
  • Technical ownership.
  • Delivery sequencing.
  • Pilot definition.

How This Project Changed the Way I Lead

  • I became more comfortable leading projects in unfamiliar technical domains.
  • I learned to use expert networks rather than trying to solve every technical question myself.
  • I became more deliberate about separating delivery leadership from technical ownership.
  • I learned that business case, TCO, architecture, and migration strategy need to align before funding and execution.
  • I learned to escalate missing specialist roles earlier.
  • I discovered that I particularly enjoy Technical Project Management roles where business, delivery, and technology intersect.

Jairo left before the migration began.
